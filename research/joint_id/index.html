TL;DR: Joint-ID restores underwater images and estimates their depth together. Two transformer U-Nets — one per task — exchange features through joint blocks built on the underwater light model, so depth helps undo the haze and the haze helps estimate depth. Trained on our synthetic dataset of 48,000 images across ten water types, the joint model beats its own single-task versions on both tasks.

Abstract

Underwater imaging is a challenging task due to factors such as scattering, absorption, and turbulence, which degrade image quality and limit visibility. In this article, we propose a novel approach for enhancing underwater images that leverages the benefits of joint learning for simultaneous image enhancement and depth estimation. We introduce Joint-ID, a transformer-based neural network that can obtain high-perceptual image quality and depth information from raw underwater images. Our approach formulates a multimodal objective function that addresses invalid depth, lack of sharpness, and image degradation based on color and local texture. We design an end-to-end training pipeline that enables joint restoration and depth estimation in a shared hierarchical feature space. In addition, we propose a synthetic dataset with various distortions and scene depths for multitask learning. We evaluate Joint-ID on synthetic and standard datasets, as well as real underwater images with diverse spectra and harsh turbidity, demonstrating its effectiveness for underwater image enhancement (UIE) and depth estimation. Furthermore, we demonstrate the ability of Joint-ID to perform feature matching and saliency detection for visually guided underwater robots. Our proposed method has the potential to improve the visual perception of underwater environments and benefit applications such as oceanography and underwater robotics.

Why Learn Them Together?

Under water, how badly an image is degraded depends on how far the scene is. McGlamery's light-propagation model writes the observed image as

Ic(x) = Jc(x)·e−βcd(x) + Bc∞·(1 − e−βcd(x))

so a full restoration needs both the distortion coefficients (Bc∞, βc) and the depth d. Image enhancement and depth estimation are usually trained separately, which throws this coupling away.

Depth tells enhancement how much was lost

Color loss and haze grow with distance and depend on the water type. A restoration network that knows the depth can estimate the transmission and ambient-light terms instead of applying one global correction.

Haze tells depth how far things are

Contrast and color attenuation are distance cues. Feeding them to the depth network helps it keep the depth scale all the way to the background, where a single-task model gives up.

Architecture

Joint-ID runs two U-Nets side by side: Single-I for enhancement and Single-D for depth. Each has a four-stage Mix Transformer++ encoder, from H/4 down to H/32, and a mix decoder. Joint blocks between the two decoders exchange features at every stage, following the light model.

Method

(A) Mix Transformer++ encoder

Reason Both tasks need global context — the absolute depth scale and the overall color cast — as well as local detail.

Method A SegFormer-style Mix Transformer with [4, 7, 12, 4] blocks per stage instead of [3, 4, 18, 4], and Rescale Layer Normalization, which keeps the feature scale and bias that standard LayerNorm discards — a known problem for dehazing. Channels: [64, 128, 320, 512].

(B) Mix decoder with dividing blocks

Reason Dense restoration and depth rely on global features more than detection or classification do, and stacked 1×1 convolutions in a deep decoder lose gradient.

Method Each decoder layer runs an MLP (global) and a CNN (local) in parallel and merges them with the encoder features; a dividing block then splits the result into a skip connection and the input of the next layer.

(C) Physics-based joint blocks

Reason The distortion coefficients are easier to estimate when the depth is known, and the depth is easier to estimate when the haze features are known.

Method The E-joint block injects Single-D's depth into Single-I through a transmission term P1 = e−βcd and ambient-light terms P2, P3 = Bc∞(1 − e−βcd); the M-joint block passes contrast and haze features from Single-I to Single-D.

(D) Multimodal objective

Reason The loss has to penalize invalid depth, missing sharpness, and color and texture degradation at the same time.

Method Enhancement uses an L2 loss plus a Laplacian edge loss, and depth uses the scale-invariant log loss S(g). Jointly: L = 2.0·(L2 + 0.1·LLap) + 1.5·√S(g).

A Synthetic Dataset for Joint Learning

No real underwater dataset gives a clean image and dense depth for every frame, so we built one. Starting from the DIML RGB-D dataset, we render underwater images with the light model for the ten Jerlov water types (I, IA, IB, II, III, 1C, 3C, 5C, 7C, and 9C). Absorption and scattering follow each water type; the green ambient light Bg is drawn from {0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7} and Br, Bb follow from it; and the distance from the surface ranges from 1.5 m to 15 m, with shorter ranges for the murkier coastal types.

Image Enhancement

We report two of our models against eight baselines (Berman et al., UWCNN, UGAN, UIEC², FUnIE-GAN, UIE-DAL, U-Shape, and DeepWave). Single-I is the enhancement network alone, trained on each benchmark like the learning-based baselines. Joint-ID is the joint model, trained only on our synthetic data. They win in different ways.

Depth Estimation

We compared with BTS, GLPDepth, and NewCRF, each pretrained on terrestrial data and then fine-tuned on our synthetic set. On the synthetic test set Joint-ID has the lowest SILog and RMS error and the highest δ1 accuracy. On real SQUID scenes it reaches SILog 12.41 and Abs Rel 0.09, the best of all models (Single-D: 14.21 and 0.19).

Does Joint Learning Help?

Training the two networks together improves both of them. With the same architecture, the joint model beats the single-task models on every enhancement and depth metric of our synthetic test set. The single models handle nearby objects but lose the depth scale toward the background.

Robot Vision

On a public underwater video, Joint-ID-enhanced frames give far more ORB inlier matches than the raw input and the other enhancement methods over most of the 200 frames, and it finds extra features in the boxed regions, where depth helps the restoration. Saliency maps on Joint-ID images also outline objects more completely than SESR, which performs enhancement, super-resolution, and saliency detection together.

What Came Next

Limitation: size and speed

Two transformer U-Nets cost 150 M parameters and run at 15.3 Hz at 256×256 on an RTX 3090 Ti (measured in the TRIDENT paper) — heavy for an underwater robot's onboard computer. Joint-ID also gives no signal of where its depth can be trusted.

Next: TRIDENT

Our follow-up TRIDENT (IEEE Sensors Journal 2025) keeps the joint idea, adds depth uncertainty, and fits all three tasks into 0.96 M parameters at 102 Hz.
